# ShortForge episode pitches

[Read the ten current narration drafts and their shot plans](EPISODE_SCRIPTS.md).

The 45–60 second cut opens on something the viewer can see and predict. Change one thing in a comparison, reveal the hidden mechanism with a cutaway, then reverse the condition and return to the opening shot. Use the same suburban set where it helps connect the episodes. Let the narration identify each object as the camera lands on it.

## 1. Why two compost bins smell different

**Opening shot.** Two matching bins take the same kitchen scraps. Open both lids in the same shot. One shows a wet mat of scraps and the other loose material mixed with dry leaves.

**Hook options**

- “Same scraps. One bin smells rotten. What changed?”
- “This handful of leaves changes what happens inside your compost.”
- “Your compost can be wet and still need air.”

**55-second cut.** 0–3s reveal both bins and ask for a prediction. 3–13s zoom into the wet mat and show its connected air gaps disappearing. 13–28s introduce dry leaves and coarse material in the comparison bin, then show microbes where air and moisture are available. 28–43s show the active core warming and the pile gradually breaking down using an elapsed-time cue. 43–55s return to the lids and pull up a handful from each bin so viewers can recognize the texture difference.

**Production detail.** Use a clear cutaway wall, separate leaf and scrap meshes, and sparse arrows in connected pore spaces. Show heat with a thermometer or thermal overlay, since a small pile may show little heating and visible steam depends on conditions. Demonstrate a damp, porous mix and keep time-to-finish out of the promise. [EPA home composting guide](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/composting-home)

## 2. Could your roof run a wind turbine?

**Opening shot.** Two identical small rotors turn in one vertical frame. One sits just above the roof ridge. The other stands in clearer air. Ask which one delivers more energy over a day.

**Hook options**

- “This turbine is spinning. Would it actually power anything?”
- “Same turbine. Same wind. Why does the one on the roof make less energy in this test?”
- “Where would you put a wind turbine if the roof were the worst spot?”

**55-second cut.** 0–5s match-cut between spinning rotors. 5–17s show the prevailing wind, then expose the wake behind the roof and tree. 17–30s place the comparison rotor in a steadier flow and animate measured power traces over the same illustrative day. 30–44s integrate each trace into a total energy counter. 44–55s pull back to reveal how wind direction and obstacles can change the comparison, then freeze on the original rooftop rotor.

**Production detail.** Show instantaneous power and accumulated energy with distinct labels. Use schematic traces without invented household savings. An actual installation depends on measured wind, siting, equipment, local rules and mounting. Keep the action on airflow and energy. [DOE small distributed wind FAQ](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/frequently-asked-questions-small-distributed-wind-systems) · [DOE small wind guidebook](https://windexchange.energy.gov/small-wind-guidebook.pdf)

## 3. The water your sink is supposed to keep

**Opening shot.** Show clear pipes under two identical sinks. A little water stays in the bend on one side. A coloured air trace rises through the dry one.

**Hook options**

- “Your sink is designed to leave this water behind.”
- “Why can a drain start smelling after nobody uses it?”
- “Which part keeps sewer air out of your kitchen?”

**50-second cut.** 0–4s freeze on the leftover water. 4–17s run a sink, follow the last portion of water into the bend and show how it closes the air path. 17–32s time-lapse evaporation in the comparison pipe. 32–42s show a separate pressure example in a vented schematic. 42–50s restore the water seal and replay the air trace stopping in the bend.

**Production detail.** Show a water seal spanning the whole pipe. Use arrows for airflow instead of visible gas in the room. Keep local plumbing requirements and diagnosis outside the script. [U.S. Public Health Service plumbing guide](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/21748/cdc_21748_DS1.pdf)

## 4. A water heater that cools the room

**Opening shot.** A warm water tank sits beside a cool air plume. “Why does a water heater blow out cold air?” Alternate hook “Where did the heat in this shower come from?”

**50-second cut.** 0–5s juxtapose the two temperatures. 5–18s track heat from room air across the evaporator. 18–34s follow refrigerant through the compressor and show heat crossing into the tank. 34–43s show the air leaving cooler. 43–50s return to the shower and the two energy inputs. A split-screen variant can compare a resistance element and the heat-pump cycle.

**Production detail.** Distinguish electricity input from heat moved from room air. Temperature and efficiency depend on the installation and operating mode. [DOE heat pump water heaters](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/hvac-heat-pump-water-heaters)

## 5. The garden that holds a storm

**Opening shot.** Rain hits two yards with the same roof area. One sends runoff straight to the street. The other briefly holds it in a planted hollow. “Why would you design your garden to flood?” Alternate hook “Can this dip in the lawn slow a downpour?”

**55-second cut.** 0–6s compare the two roof downspouts. 6–20s follow runoff through a shallow depression. 20–36s cut below grade to show temporary storage and infiltration. 36–48s show what happens during a larger storm using an overflow route. 48–55s return to the same two street inlets as the storm clears.

**Production detail.** Use rainfall of equal intensity in both halves. Soil and site conditions govern drainage. A separate variant can show an underdrain where one is part of the design. [EPA green infrastructure examples](https://www.epa.gov/green-infrastructure/types-green-infrastructure)

## 6. Where induction heat starts

**Opening shot.** Water boils in a pan over a black glass surface. Cut through the cooktop to reveal a copper coil. “Where does the heat start?” Alternate hook “Why can one pan heat on this stove while another barely reacts?”

**50-second cut.** 0–5s show the boil and reveal the coil. 5–21s animate the alternating magnetic field. 21–34s show induced currents heating a compatible pan. 34–43s swap to an incompatible pan for the comparison. 43–50s use a thermal view to show the hot pan warming the glass beneath it.

**Production detail.** Use a thermal overlay for energy transfer and never demonstrate safety by touching a just-used surface. [DOE induction cooktop guide](https://www.energy.gov/articles/making-switch-induction-stoves-or-cooktops)

## 7. The nearly invisible coating on a window

**Opening shot.** Two windows look alike to the eye and behave differently in a heat-flow cutaway. “Can two windows look identical and pass different amounts of heat?” Alternate hook “Which surface inside the glass changes how heat crosses?”

**55-second cut.** 0–5s reveal matching windows. 5–17s pull a pane apart into the glass, gap and coated surface. 17–31s show the coating reducing long-wave radiative heat transfer. 31–45s switch to the solar-gain question, showing why a window specification also considers incoming sun. 45–55s collapse both panes back into the same visual.

**Production detail.** Give visible light and infrared separate treatments. Low-E products vary in solar heat gain. Avoid presenting all coatings as equivalent. [DOE high-performance window glass](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/high-performance-window-systems-glass) · [DOE window performance ratings](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-residential-windows-doors-and-skylights)

## 8. When opening a second window helps a house cool

**Opening shot.** In a dollhouse, one open window shows little airflow in the test. Opening a second changes the path. “Why did opening this upstairs window change what happened downstairs?” Alternate hook “Which way would air move if the wind stopped?”

**55-second cut.** 0–6s show the before and after. 6–20s use a cool night, low inlet and high outlet to reveal buoyancy-driven flow. 20–36s switch on a crosswind and show pressure across opposite sides. 36–47s reverse outdoor conditions or wind direction and change the arrows. 47–55s return to the first opening and ask the viewer to identify where the air enters.

**Production detail.** Label indoor and outdoor temperatures. Keep cooling conditional on weather and use separate comparisons for wind and buoyancy. [DOE natural ventilation and cooling](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/natural-ventilation-and-cooling)

## 9. Why a siphon can send water uphill first

**Opening shot.** Follow water over the high bend in a transparent tube. “The water climbs first. What’s pulling it through?” Alternate hook “I raised one end of this tube and the flow stopped. Which end?”

**45-second cut.** 0–4s show the water cresting the bend. 4–15s pull back to expose the source surface and lower outlet. 15–30s follow the continuous filled column and reveal the overall elevation drop. 30–39s lift the outlet to source level, then show the effect of an air break in a separate shot. 39–45s reset the outlet and loop to the high bend.

**Production detail.** Mark both elevations. Use a prefilled demonstration tube and an unbroken liquid column for the operating case. Reuse the transparent plumbing kit from the drain episode. [Princeton siphon analysis](https://kirkmcd.princeton.edu/examples/siphon.pdf)

## 10. Why two roofs heat up differently in the same sun

**Opening shot.** Two houses share the same sunlight. Switch to a thermal-style view and one roof surface lights up more. “Same sun. Why does one roof heat up faster?” Alternate hook “Is the colour you can see enough to tell how hot a roof gets?”

**50-second cut.** 0–5s reveal the difference. 5–18s track reflected and absorbed sunlight. 18–33s visualize how the roof also gives off heat. 33–43s cut into the attic and introduce insulation to show why the room response can differ from the roof surface response. 43–50s return to the matching roofs and replay the sunbeams.

**Production detail.** Match sun exposure and roof assembly during the comparison. Label the thermal colours as illustrative and avoid promised degrees or bill savings. [DOE cool roof guide](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2013/10/f3/coolroofguide.pdf)

## First production sequence

1. **Compost.** Open on the two bins with the same scraps. Preview the wet mat, air path and warm core before scripting the final takes. The bin comparison is readable without sound and leaves room for one practical payoff.
2. **Home wind.** Reuse the solar street and roof. Make the opening rotors turn similarly so the daily-energy reveal answers a real question. Keep power traces and the accumulating totals in the same shot.
3. **Sink trap.** Reuse the water assets and keep the frame on the water seal. This can share a transparent-pipe kit with the siphon episode and establish a repeatable “hidden inside the house” series.

**Opening tests.** Compare the compost lid reveal with a close shot of two handfuls. For wind, try two turning rotors against one rotor with a falling power trace. For the trap, compare the final drop against the dry and full air-path demo.
