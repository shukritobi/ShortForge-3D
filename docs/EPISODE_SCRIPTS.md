# ShortForge episode scripts

Ten first-draft voiceovers for vertical 51–56 second episodes. Each row pairs the spoken line with the image that should be on screen. Use the times as edit targets. Measure the full takes before locking the cuts. Start with the director cut's single 1.07× voice setting and revise the lines or the cut lengths when a beat needs more room.

## 1. The bin you regret opening

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | Same scraps. One bin smells rotten. What changed? | Two lids pop. The gardener recoils from one and pauses at the other. |
| 4–13s | Both started with yesterday’s vegetable peels. But look through the wall. On this side, wet scraps have sunk into a mat. | Peel away matching bin walls. Match-cut from loose peels falling to the settled wet mat. |
| 13–26s | Those dark pockets are where air struggles to get in. Now mix the second batch with dry leaves and coarse stalks. See the little spaces that stay open? | Follow air arrows until they stop. Add browns to the comparison and track air through the connected gaps. |
| 26–39s | Microbes use that oxygen while they break down the scraps. In an active pile, their work can even warm the middle. Give it time and the pieces start disappearing. | Cut to a small thermometer at the core. Time-lapse the shapes breaking down, with a visible time jump. |
| 39–53s | Next time a bin smells bad, look before you add anything. Is it damp and loose, or have the scraps packed together? Lift the lid again. You can see the difference. | Compare a loose handful with a compact clump. Match back to the two lids. |

Use an illustrative thermometer for the active pile. Show moisture in both bins and vary the structure with coarse browns. [EPA home composting guide](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/composting-home)

## 2. The turbine that fooled me

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | Both are spinning. Which turbine wins by sunset? | Split frame with matched rotors and a sunrise-to-sunset clock. Hide the totals. |
| 4–14s | I’d have picked the roof. It’s the highest point on the house. Then I watched what the wind did just past the ridge. | Follow one ribbon over the roof. The camera crosses the ridge with it and reveals the wake. |
| 14–28s | The wind bends around the house and breaks into a rough wake. The roof rotor gets bursts of wind. This second rotor stands in a steadier stream in our test. | Reveal the same incoming wind approaching both sites. Show variable and steadier flow at rotor height. |
| 28–41s | Here’s where the spinning fooled me. Watch the power traces. One jumps and drops. The other keeps producing between gusts. Add up the energy over the same hours. | Reveal schematic power traces above energy counters. Advance the clock and accumulate each total. |
| 41–54s | Now you can see how much energy each site delivered. Change the wind direction and the result may change again. Where would you measure the wind first? | Reveal the totals, then rotate the wind arrow and watch the wake move. End on the two rotors again. |

Use clearly illustrative traces without a kilowatt-hour promise. The result belongs to the simulated siting comparison. [DOE small distributed wind FAQ](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/frequently-asked-questions-small-distributed-wind-systems)

## 3. The house kept a little water

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | The guest bathroom smells strange. Look under the sink. | A guest opens the door. Drop through the vanity as the room falls out of frame. |
| 4–16s | Watch the last of this water. Most of it heads away, but the bend keeps a little pool. The sink was designed to do that. | Follow the last slug down the clear pipe. Hold on water sealing the whole cross-section. |
| 16–28s | It’s called a trap. See this air coming from farther down the pipe? The pool seals the passage, so the air has no open route through the drain. | Trace air from the drain side and stop the arrows at the water. |
| 28–40s | Now jump forward. Nobody’s used the guest sink for weeks. As the water evaporates, a little gap opens over it. Follow the same air this time. | Calendar pages pass. Let the meniscus drop below the sealing point, then run the same arrows again. |
| 40–53s | When water runs again, the bend refills and the path closes. The smallest part of the whole scene was the part doing the work. Watch that last drop. | Refill the trap. Freeze on the restored seal as the air trace stops. Loop to the first draining shot. |

The story presents a dry trap as one cause of an odour. Use an airflow graphic and a time jump with no universal evaporation time. [U.S. Public Health Service plumbing guide](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/21748/cdc_21748_DS1.pdf)

## 4. The missing heat in the laundry room

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | Why does a water heater blow cold air? | Match-cut from steam at the shower to a cool exhaust plume beside the tank. |
| 4–14s | Follow the air into the top of the tank. It passes over a coil, and some of its heat moves into a refrigerant. | Track one air ribbon through the evaporator. Move the heat marker onto the refrigerant side. |
| 14–26s | The compressor uses electricity to squeeze that refrigerant. Its temperature rises, so now it’s hot enough to give up heat to the water inside the tank. | Cut into the compressor, then follow the hot refrigerant to the tank's heat exchanger. |
| 26–40s | Look back at the air we started with. It lost heat at the first coil, so it comes out cooler. Meanwhile the water picks up heat at the other end. | Show both heat-transfer points in one split frame. Keep the water and refrigerant paths separate. |
| 40–53s | The shower gets heat from the room, with electricity doing the work of moving it. That cold breeze was the clue to where your hot water got its heat. | Run the full transfer in a single pullback from room to tank to shower. Return to the cool plume. |

Show a unit in heat-pump mode and distinguish energy drawn from the room from electrical input. [DOE heat-pump water heaters](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/hvac-heat-pump-water-heaters)

## 5. The yard that floods on purpose

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3s | My neighbour built this puddle on purpose. | Storm hits two matching roofs. One downspout feeds a planted depression. |
| 3–15s | The roofs get the same rain. On this side, water runs straight past the lawn and into the street. On the other side, it stops here. | Track matching water streams. Hold on the difference when one reaches the curb. |
| 15–27s | That shallow dip is a rain garden. It holds some runoff long enough for water to soak into the ground. See it spreading below the plants? | Cut below grade. Show a temporary pool and the wetting front moving downward. |
| 27–38s | Now turn up the storm. Once the garden fills, the extra water still needs a way out. This one sends overflow along a separate path. | Raise the rain rate until water reaches a visible overflow notch. Follow the route. |
| 38–52s | After the rain, one yard has already sent most of its runoff down the street. The other is still letting some of it soak in. That puddle bought time. | Drop the rain rate to zero. Show the second pool slowly receding. Match back to the initial split screen. |

Match roof area and rainfall. Show the outcome for this soil and layout, with a visible overflow route. [EPA types of green infrastructure](https://www.epa.gov/green-infrastructure/types-green-infrastructure)

## 6. The burner you can’t see

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | The water’s boiling. Where did the heat start? | Hold on rolling bubbles and smooth black glass. Freeze as the camera begins to descend. |
| 4–16s | Under the glass is a copper coil. Switch it on and a changing magnetic field reaches up through the glass into the bottom of the pan. | Reveal the coil. Animate the field through the glass, using translucent contours. |
| 16–29s | That changing field drives electric currents around inside the metal. The metal resists those currents and heats up. Now watch the heat spread from the pan into the water. | Zoom into the compatible base. Trace current loops, then switch to a thermal view as heat moves into the water. |
| 29–42s | Put a pan with an incompatible base in the same spot and it may barely heat at all. Same coil. The difference is how the base responds to the field. | Swap pans in the same shot. Label the bases and keep the same coil in view. |
| 42–56s | Lift the boiling pan and you’ll still see a warm patch in a thermal view. Heat moved back into the glass from the pan. The pan was doing the heating. | Move the pan clear and reveal a residual heat patch in the glass. Match-cut back to the opening bubbles. |

Use a thermal overlay to show residual heat. Frame the compatible cookware base and the direction of heat transfer. [DOE induction cooktops](https://www.energy.gov/articles/making-switch-induction-stoves-or-cooktops)

## 7. The window layer you can’t see

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | Same window. Why does one leak more heat? | Two lit windows side by side on a cold evening. Keep the external finish and frame matched. |
| 4–15s | Wait until the sun is down. Inside, the room still sends out heat as infrared radiation. Watch what happens when it reaches the glass. | Fade to night. Separate indoor infrared arrows from the daylight layer, then push through to the glass section. |
| 15–26s | Pull the panes apart. Here’s the difference. A nearly invisible coating on one surface reduces how much heat is exchanged by radiation across the gap. | Explode matching double glazing and highlight the low-E surface. Reduce the net infrared flow on the coated side. |
| 26–38s | By morning, both still let in visible light. But how much warmth comes in with the sun is a separate question. Different coatings handle it differently. | Time-lapse to morning. Run a distinct sunlight overlay and give two coatings different solar-gain treatments. |
| 38–51s | So the next time two windows look the same, ask two questions. How easily does heat cross the window? And how much heat does the sun bring in? | Return the panes to the frames. Reveal the labels `U-factor` and `Solar heat gain` next to the corresponding arrows. |

Use distinct visual treatments for room-temperature infrared and incoming solar radiation. [DOE high-performance window glass](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/high-performance-window-systems-glass) · [DOE window performance ratings](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/purchasing-energy-efficient-residential-windows-doors-and-skylights)

## 8. The upstairs window that changed downstairs

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | Opening this upstairs window changed the room downstairs. | Warm-room thermometer. A downstairs curtain barely moves until the upstairs sash opens. |
| 4–16s | Turn the wind off for a moment. The air indoors is warmer and lighter. Give it a way out up high and it can rise and leave. | Pause outdoor wind. Reveal the cross-section and track warm air up the stairs to the high opening. |
| 16–28s | Now look downstairs. As warm air leaves upstairs, cooler air can enter through the low window to replace it. We’ve made a path through the house. | Follow a cool ribbon entering at the low opening. Show both openings in one cutaway. |
| 28–41s | Bring the breeze back. Now wind adds a push on one side of the house. Change its direction, and the air may take a different route through the rooms. | Toggle wind on. Rotate its direction and remap arrows through openings without moving the camera. |
| 41–54s | When you open a window to cool down, picture the whole trip. Where will the air get in? Where can it leave? That upstairs window changed both rooms. | Trace the route in one smooth pass and end back at the downstairs curtain. |

The buoyancy demo uses a cooler outdoor night. Keep wind and buoyancy separate before showing them together. [DOE natural ventilation and cooling](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/natural-ventilation-and-cooling)

## 9. The water that climbs first

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | Water’s climbing uphill. Which end would you lift? | Follow a dyed band climbing through a transparent tube. Keep the outlet hidden for the first question. |
| 4–15s | Pull back. The tube is already full, and the outlet is lower than the water surface in the first container. The whole trip goes downhill. | Reveal the free outlet and draw both elevation lines, leaving the crest visible. |
| 15–28s | Gravity drives the flow over that drop. The tube stays full, so as water leaves the outlet, more water moves up the other leg to replace it. | Track equal markers through the tube. Keep the elevation difference highlighted. |
| 28–41s | I’ll raise the outlet until it’s level with the source surface. Now the height difference is gone and the flow stops. Lower it again while the tube stays full. | Lift the outlet slowly and hold as flow stops. Lower it and restart the stream. |
| 41–52s | You see the water climb again. This time you know where to look. The answer was down at the outlet, just outside the first frame. | Match back to the opening at the bend, then briefly reveal the outlet again for the loop. |

Use a prefilled tube with a free outlet and an intact liquid column. Hold the outlet at source level for the no-head shot. [Princeton siphon analysis](https://kirkmcd.princeton.edu/examples/siphon.pdf)

## 10. A hot roof and a cool room

| Time | Narration | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4s | One roof is hotter. What about the room? | Show two roofs under identical sunlight. A surface thermal overlay separates them. |
| 4–15s | Give the roofs the same sun. One surface reflects more of it, so less is absorbed. The difference shows up first on the outside. | Follow matching sunbeams. Count reflected versus absorbed rays with schematic arrows. |
| 15–27s | A roof can also give off heat as infrared. Between what it takes in and what it gives back, the two surfaces can settle at different temperatures. | Switch from incoming sunlight to emitted infrared. Hold both surface readings as illustrative relative bars. |
| 27–40s | Now cut through the attic. The room is farther down the heat path. Change the insulation under one roof and you change how readily that heat reaches the room. | Slice into the attic and ceiling. Keep the roof finish fixed while changing only the insulation in a second comparison. |
| 40–54s | So a roof temperature tells you what happened at the surface. To explain the room, follow the heat through the rest of the house. Now look at those roofs again. | Hold roof and room indicators in the same frame. Retrace the heat path and return to the opening thermal view. |

Match both roof assemblies for the surface comparison. Change insulation only in the second pass. Label the thermal overlay as illustrative. [DOE cool roof guide](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2013/10/f3/coolroofguide.pdf)
